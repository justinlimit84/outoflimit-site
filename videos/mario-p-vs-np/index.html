Super Mario Is Secretly a Million Dollar Math Problem (P vs NP)

4 Dec 2026 8 min 9 s Limits

One plumber, one flag, one question: can he reach it? For a big enough level, nobody knows a fast way for a computer to answer. The reason is P vs NP, the million-dollar question that the locks on your bank login quietly bet on. In 2014, four computer scientists showed how to hide that question inside a Super Mario Bros. level.

The story in 60 seconds

A computer can always answer "can this level be beaten?" by trying every path. The trouble is how fast the paths multiply. So the real question is whether there's a shortcut.

Computer scientists sort problems exactly like that. Problems a computer can solve quickly are in P. Problems where a proposed answer can be checked quickly are in NP. Everything in P is in NP. The open question, one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize Problems with a million dollars attached, is the reverse: if an answer is quick to check, is it always quick to find? Most experts think not. Nobody has proved it.

In 1971 Stephen Cook, in Toronto, showed that one puzzle, Boolean satisfiability (SAT), is a "master puzzle": every NP problem can be translated into it. Leonid Levin found the same idea independently in the Soviet Union. In 1972 Richard Karp showed that 21 well-known problems are all that hard. They got the name NP-complete. The best-known form of the master puzzle, 3-SAT, is a pizza order: each friend states three wishes and is happy if at least one comes true. Can one pizza make everyone happy?

Greg Aloupis, Erik Demaine, Alan Guo and Giovanni Viglietta built that puzzle out of Super Mario Bros. pieces. A power-up at the start makes Mario big, and only big Mario can break the bricks before the flag. Each topping becomes a one-way drop: fall down the left passage (yes) or the right one (no), and you can't jump back up. Each friend becomes a room where a star is freed when Mario passes through. The last corridor is lined with fire bars, and Mario only survives it if a star is waiting in every room. A crossover lets two paths cross without mixing.

So the flag can be reached exactly when the puzzle has an answer, and Mario's route is the answer. A fast way to decide "can Mario win?" would be a fast way to solve every NP problem. That's what NP-hard means here: not hard to play, hard to predict.

Small puzzles are easy: the video's has 5 answers among its 8 choices (no toppings at all is one of them). The harder one has exactly one answer among 32. "Try every choice" counts through them in order, the slow way. Nobody knows a method that stays fast as the toppings grow, and that is the P vs NP question. The lab is tested: the video's two tries, the single answer of the harder puzzle, and every number shown.

Harder, and easier, than we thought

In 2016, Erik Demaine, Giovanni Viglietta and Aaron Williams went further: with cleverer gadgets, deciding a generalized Super Mario Bros. level is PSPACE-complete, a class believed to be even harder than NP. Their gadgets also survive the game's known glitches. But there's a catch, and it's in the same paper: the proofs need levels of any size and either an arbitrarily large screen or a game that remembers every enemy off screen. The real games forget what's off screen. With a fixed screen and few sprites, the problem becomes solvable in polynomial time again. So no level you've played is secretly a million-dollar puzzle. The hardness is in what the rules could build.

Why your bank login cares

Public-key encryption relies on problems that are easy to check but believed hard to solve. As the computer scientist Lance Fortnow points out, a proof that P = NP with a practical algorithm would make public-key cryptography impossible. The same discovery would also make countless planning and design problems easy. Either way, it's not just a math prize.

Read the full narration

Can he win?

Here's a simple level. One plumber. One flag. One question: can he reach it? Easy, right? Now make the level bigger. Every jump doubles the paths to check. Build it big enough, and nobody knows a fast way for a computer to answer that question. The reason is the same unsolved puzzle that the locks on your bank login quietly bet on. It's called P versus NP. It's over fifty years old, and there's a million dollars for whoever solves it. In twenty fourteen, a team of computer scientists proved that Super Mario Bros. is secretly one of these puzzles. We'll build a Mario level that's really a logic problem in disguise.

Too many paths

Every level Nintendo shipped can be beaten. Designers made sure of it. But imagine any level you could build from the game's pieces: blocks, power-ups, fire bars. Of any size. Can a computer look at it and say "yes, beatable" or "no, impossible"? Sure. Try every path. Jump here, wait there, run past the enemy. Every jump splits the paths in two. Eighty jumps deep, that's about a trillion trillion paths. Checking every path one by one is hopeless. The real question is: is there a shortcut?

Check or find: P and NP

Here's a filled-in Sudoku. Is it correct? You scan the rows, the columns, the boxes. Seconds. Here's a blank one. Solve it. Now you're guessing, backtracking, erasing. Minutes, sometimes hours. Computer scientists sort problems exactly like this. If a computer can solve a problem quickly, it's in a group called P. If a computer can check an answer quickly, it's in a group called NP. Every quick-to-solve problem is also quick to check. The million-dollar question is the other way round: if you can check an answer fast, can you always find it fast too? Hold on to that, because your bank cares a lot about the answer. Almost every expert thinks the answer is no. But nobody has proved it.

The master puzzle

The story starts in nineteen seventy-one, with a mathematician in Toronto named Stephen Cook. Cook found something astonishing: one puzzle that every NP problem can be translated into. Solve that one puzzle fast, and you solve all of them fast. Across the Iron Curtain, Leonid Levin in the Soviet Union found the same idea on his own. A year later, Richard Karp showed that twenty-one famous problems, from scheduling to route planning, were all the same puzzle in disguise. These "hardest" problems got a name: NP complete. Its most famous form is called three-SAT. Let's play it. Three friends are ordering one pizza. Each friend has a rule with three wishes, and they're happy if at least one wish comes true. Friend one: "mushrooms, or olives, or no pineapple." Friend two: "olives, or peppers, or no mushrooms." And so on. Can you choose toppings that make every friend happy at the same time? Check a choice: easy. Find one, with hundreds of toppings and thousands of friends: that's where it gets brutal. To prove a new problem is just as hard, researchers show that any three-SAT puzzle can be rebuilt inside it. In twenty fourteen, Greg Aloupis, Erik Demaine, Alan Guo and Giovanni Viglietta did that with Super Mario Bros.

The machine inside Mario

Their idea: turn the level into a machine. Paths become wires, and little rooms become logic. It starts with a power-up. Mario grows big, and he'll need to stay big to break the bricks in front of the flag at the very end. Next, a choice. Mario drops down the left passage or the right one. The ledge is too high to jump back up. Left means "mushrooms: yes". Right means "mushrooms: no". One drop, and the decision is locked forever. Every friend gets a little room, and each passage leads past the rooms of the friends it makes happy. When Mario passes through, he hits a block and frees a star, and the star stays bouncing there. A room gets a star only if Mario came through one of its three doors: that friend got at least one wish. At the end, there's one last corridor full of fire bars. It passes by every room. Mario only survives if a star is waiting in every room, because each star makes him invincible for the next stretch. Miss one friend, and he burns. There's even a bridge piece, so two wires can cross without mixing up.

Build one: a pizza order becomes a level

Let's build one together. A smaller pizza: three toppings, three friends. Friend one wants mushrooms, or olives, or no pineapple. Friend two wants no mushrooms, or no olives, or pineapple. Friend three wants no mushrooms, or olives, or pineapple. Step one: every topping becomes a choice. Three drops in a row: mushrooms, olives, pineapple. Step two: every friend becomes a room, waiting for a star. Step three: wire them up. The "mushrooms: yes" passage runs past friend one's room, because mushrooms make friend one happy. The "mushrooms: no" passage runs past friends two and three. Every wish gets its own wire. Now let's play. Mushrooms yes, olives no, pineapple no. Friend one gets a star. Friend two gets a star. But friend three's room stays empty. In the fire bar corridor, that empty room is fatal. This choice fails. Try again. Mushrooms yes, olives no, pineapple yes. Now every room has a star. Mario runs the whole corridor, breaks the bricks, and reaches the flag. And look at the route he took. It spells out the answer: mushrooms and pineapple, no olives. Every friend is happy. Finding that route was easy here. With a thousand toppings, nobody knows a fast way. Put it together, and any three-SAT puzzle becomes one giant Mario level. If the flag can be reached, the puzzle has a solution, and Mario's route is the answer. If it can't, there's no solution. So a fast way to decide "can Mario win?" would be a fast way to solve every NP problem. That's what "Mario is NP hard" means. Not that the game is hard to play. That it's hard to predict.

Plot twist: harder, and easier

Two years later, Demaine, Viglietta and Aaron Williams went further. With cleverer gadgets, they showed generalized Mario is even harder: PSPACE complete. Think of it as problems where even checking an answer could take an astronomically long time. But there's a catch. These proofs need gigantic levels, and either a gigantic screen or a game that remembers every enemy off screen. The real game forgets what's off screen. With that limit, the same paper shows the problem becomes easy again. So no level you've played is secretly a million-dollar puzzle. The hardness hides in what the rules could build.

The lock on your bank login

Remember the lock on your bank login? Online banking, messaging, and the padlock in your browser all rely on puzzles that are easy to check but believed hard to solve. If someone proved P equals NP with a practical method, the computer scientist Lance Fortnow points out, that whole kind of lock would stop working. But the same discovery would let us plan perfect delivery routes and solve countless design problems overnight. So it's not just a math prize. It's a bet the whole internet makes every day.

The question for you

A cheerful plumber, a pizza order, and the locks on your bank all share one unanswered question. If you want to go further, look up Cook's nineteen seventy-one paper, Karp's twenty-one problems, and "Classic Nintendo Games are Computationally Hard". The authors even published their gadget levels. So here's my question for you: if someone proved P equals NP tomorrow, should they publish it, or keep it secret? Tell me in the comments.
