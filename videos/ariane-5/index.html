The Ariane 5 Explosion: The Number That Was Too Big for a Rocket

28 Nov 2026 8 min 37 s Breaks

On 4 June 1996, Europe's brand-new Ariane 5 tore itself apart 39 seconds after ignition, on its very first flight. The weather was fine, the engines were fine, and nothing hit it. It was brought down by one number that was too big for the space a program had left for it — and the backup computer, running the same program, failed in exactly the same way.

The story in 60 seconds

To fly straight, a rocket has to know every instant which way it's pointing and how fast it's moving. On Ariane 5 that was the job of the inertial reference system — laser gyros and accelerometers with a small computer inside — and there were two of them, one active and one on hot standby, with identical hardware and software.

Inside both, a piece of software called alignment was still running after lift-off. It only makes sense while the rocket stands still on the pad; the Inquiry Board said that once the launcher lifts off it "serves no purpose". It kept running for about 40 seconds of flight anyway, because the software was shared with Ariane 4, where it had a use. And Ariane 5's flight path built up horizontal speed five times faster than Ariane 4's.

At 36.7 seconds into the flight, a value called the horizontal bias got too big for that 16-bit space. In Ada, the language this code was written in, that raises an operand error. The engineers had found seven conversions that could overflow and protected four of them; three were left unprotected, partly to keep the computer under an 80% workload target and because the values were believed to be physically limited. For the horizontal bias, that belief came from Ariane 4's trajectory — and Ariane 5's trajectory had never been written into the box's requirements.

The rule for any exception was: report it, save the details, and shut the processor down — sensible for a burnt-out chip, useless against a software bug that strikes every copy at the same moment. Worse, the active unit's diagnostic pattern was read by the main computer as if it were real flight data. It swung the boosters' nozzles, then the main engine's, to full deflection, to correct a turn that had never happened.

Three chances to catch it

The reviews. The decision to leave three conversions unprotected was agreed, but the justification never appeared in the source code, so outside reviewers couldn't see it. The box tests. The unit was tested hard for its environment, but never with Ariane 5's trajectory fed into it. The full rehearsal. In 1992 the project decided not to put the real guidance units into the closed-loop simulation of the whole flight; software stand-ins took their place. The board's verdict: had the system been included, the failure could have been detected.

The rules it left behind

The Inquiry Board's report, published on 19 July 1996, made 14 recommendations. Four of them, in plain words:

  • R1: no software function should run during flight unless it's needed.
  • R3: don't let a sensor stop sending best-effort data.
  • R5: identify every implicit assumption in the code, and verify the range of values every variable can take.
  • R2 and R10: test with as much real equipment as possible, closed-loop, with the real trajectory in the specifications and tests.

"…software should be assumed to be faulty until applying the currently accepted best practice methods can demonstrate that it is correct." — Ariane 501 Inquiry Board, 1996

Today, static analysers can prove before a program ever runs that no value overflows its space: in November 2003, the Astrée analyser proved that the primary flight-control software of the Airbus A340 had no run-time errors at all. And Ariane 5 itself, once fixed, flew 117 times between 1996 and 2023.

Transcript

Read the full narration

Thirty-nine seconds

What do Europe's brand-new rocket and the number thirty-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-seven have in common? This is the Ariane 5 explosion. 4 June 1996, the rocket's very first flight. Thirty-nine seconds after ignition, it tore itself apart, about four kilometres up. The weather was fine. The engines were fine. Nothing hit it. It was brought down by one number that was too big to fit in the space a program had left for it. Today: how that happened, why the backup computer couldn't save it, and the rule it taught every engineer who writes flight software.

Two guardians

Ariane 5 was Europe's new heavy rocket: a giant core stage with one main engine, called Vulcain, and two solid boosters strapped to its sides. Flight 501 lifted off from Kourou, Europe's spaceport on the coast of South America. That morning the countdown was held for an hour, only because visibility wasn't good enough yet. Then it went. To fly straight, a rocket has to know, every instant, which way it's pointing and how fast it's moving. That's the job of a box called the inertial reference system. Laser gyros feel every turn. Accelerometers feel every push. A small computer inside turns them into angles and speeds. It sends those numbers to the rocket's main computer, which steers by swivelling the nozzles of the engines. If that box goes blind, the rocket goes blind. So Ariane 5 carried two of them, running side by side: one in charge, one on hot standby, ready to take over instantly. Two of everything. It's the oldest safety rule in engineering: if one part breaks, its twin takes over. But there was a catch hiding in the word "identical", and we'll get to it at thirty-six point seven seconds.

A job from another rocket

For the first thirty seconds, Flight 501 was perfect. But inside both guidance boxes, a piece of software was running that had no reason to be on. Before launch, the box has to find out exactly where it is and which way is down. That's called alignment. It only works while the rocket is standing still on the pad. It's like a bathroom scale trying to zero itself while you're jumping up and down on it. Once the rocket lifts off, the board that investigated later put it bluntly: this function "serves no purpose." Yet it kept running for about forty seconds into the flight. Why? Because the software came from Ariane 4, the rocket before it. On Ariane 4, keeping alignment running a little longer meant that if the countdown was stopped in the last seconds, the launch could restart quickly, without a forty-five-minute realignment. It had actually been used, once, in 1989. Ariane 5 didn't need that feature at all. It was kept so the two rockets could share the same, proven software. Hand-me-down shoes, for a sibling who runs much faster. And Ariane 5 did run faster. Sideways. Its flight path built up horizontal speed five times faster than Ariane 4's did. And the alignment software was quietly measuring that horizontal speed the whole time.

The number that didn't fit

Deep in the alignment code was one value called the horizontal bias. The computer held it as a 64-bit floating-point number: a format that can store anything from tiny fractions to astronomically large values. But at one point, the code copied it into a 16-bit integer: a whole number with room for nothing bigger than thirty-two thousand, seven hundred and sixty-seven. Picture a car-park ticket machine with only four digit slots: the moment the fee needs a fifth, it doesn't print the wrong amount. It jams. That's what this computer's language, Ada, did when a number didn't fit. It raised an error, called an operand error. At thirty-six point seven seconds into the flight, the horizontal bias got too big to fit. Here's what makes it painful. The engineers had checked for exactly this. They found seven conversions that could overflow, and added protection to four of them. Three were left unprotected, to keep the computer's workload under a target of eighty percent, and because those values were believed to be physically limited, with plenty of margin. For the horizontal bias, that belief came from Ariane 4's flight path. And the Ariane 5 flight path had never been written into the box's requirements.

Same code, same bug

So what happens when the guidance computer hits an error? The rules said: report the failure, save the details to memory, and shut the processor down. That rule made sense for broken hardware: a burnt-out chip should step aside, and let its twin take over. The backup box hit the error first, and shut itself down. Seventy-two thousandths of a second later, one data cycle, the active box hit the very same error. And shut down too. Remember "identical"? Both boxes ran the same software, fed the same numbers. A twin with the same bug is like carrying two copies of the same wrong map. As the board wrote, two perfectly healthy units had switched themselves off. A bug isn't random like a burnt-out chip. It strikes every copy, at the same moment.

An error message steering a rocket

And now the strangest part: the rocket didn't simply lose its guidance. As it shut down, the active box sent out a diagnostic pattern, its error report. The main computer read that error report as if it were real flight data. Imagine an autopilot reading an error code off its own screen, and taking it as the altitude. To fix a turn that had never happened, it swung the boosters' nozzles, and then the main engine's, all the way to the stops. The rocket slewed sideways into the air rushing past it, at an angle of more than twenty degrees. The air tore the boosters away from the core, and that triggered the rocket's self-destruct, exactly as designed. Thirty-nine seconds. Gone. The debris rained over twelve square kilometres of mangrove swamp and savanna, and investigators waded in to find both guidance boxes. Their memories, plus computer simulations, replayed the whole chain of events exactly.

Three chances to catch it

The hardest question for the board wasn't what happened. It was why nobody caught it first. There were three chances. The first was the design reviews. The decision to leave three values unprotected was agreed, but the reasoning behind it never appeared in the code, so outside reviewers couldn't see it. The second was testing the box itself. It was tested hard, for heat and vibration and much more. But never with Ariane 5's real flight path fed into it. The third was the full rehearsal: a giant simulation of the whole flight with real equipment in the loop. Except the two guidance boxes. In 1992 they were swapped for software stand-ins, because they were considered already qualified. The board's verdict on that choice was simple: had the real system been included, the failure could have been detected. Every check was done carefully. Each one just assumed that somebody else had covered the rocket's real flight path.

Fourteen rules

Six weeks later, an independent board published its report. Engineers still read it today. Its verdict: specification and design errors in the software. It made fourteen recommendations. Here are four of them. One: no software should run in flight unless it's needed. Two: a sensor should never go silent; it should keep sending its best guess. Three: check the range of every value a program can take, against the real hardware and the real flight path. Four: test the real equipment, together, with the real trajectory in the loop. But the line that changed the culture was this: software should be "assumed to be faulty" until it's shown to be correct. Innocent until proven guilty, turned around. Today, there are tools that read flight code and prove, before it ever flies, that no number can overflow its space. In 2003, one of them, called Astrée, proved that the main flight-control software of an Airbus airliner had no run-time errors at all. And Ariane 5? Once fixed, it flew a hundred and seventeen times, through 2023.

The twist

Here's the twist. Nothing in Flight 501 was broken. Both computers did exactly what they'd been told to do. They just hadn't been told which rocket they were flying.
