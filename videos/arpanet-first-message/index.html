The First Internet Message Crashed After Two Letters (ARPANET 1969)

7 Dec 2026 6 min 9 s Breaks

On 29 October 1969, a student at UCLA tried to log in to a computer at the Stanford Research Institute, 350 miles away. He meant to type LOGIN. "L" and "O" arrived, and then the computer at SRI crashed. So the first message on the network that became the internet was two letters long. The way those letters travelled, cut into packets, is still how every message you send gets where it's going.

The story in 60 seconds

The network was ARPANET, paid for by the research agency of the US Defense Department so that researchers could use expensive computers somewhere else. The first two sites were UCLA, with an SDS Sigma 7, and SRI, with an SDS 940. Beside each computer stood a new cabinet, an IMP (Interface Message Processor), built by BBN from a Honeywell 516 minicomputer with about 12,000 words of memory. Its only job was moving messages. A leased phone line joined the two IMPs at 50,000 bits a second.

Charley Kline at UCLA and Bill Duvall at SRI were also on an ordinary phone call. Charley typed L. His computer handed it to the IMP, which wrapped it in a message with a header saying where it was going and sent it up the line, one bit at a time. The IMP at SRI checked that it had arrived intact, said so, and handed it to its computer. "Got the L." Same for the O. Then he typed the G, and the computer at SRI crashed.

What kept working

Every letter arrived. The crash was in the computer at the far end, not in the network. The IMPs were built expecting the phone line to make mistakes: every packet carried check digits worked out from its bits, the receiving IMP did the same calculation, and anything that didn't match was thrown away. The sending IMP, with no acknowledgement, sent it again. The 1970 paper describing the IMP puts the principle plainly: the IMP must keep working "whether the Host is functioning properly or not".

Why packets

A phone call in 1969 held a whole circuit open for as long as you talked, even in the silences. Computers don't talk like that: they send a burst, then nothing, then another burst. In the 1960s Paul Baran at RAND and Donald Davies at Britain's National Physical Laboratory reached the same answer independently: small blocks, each carrying its destination, each routed on its own. Many conversations can share one line, and if a route breaks, packets take another.

Store and forward

Inside the network every IMP does the same three things: it stores the whole packet, checks it and passes it on, and keeps its copy until the next one confirms. When an outgoing line is busy, packets wait in a queue. How long they wait, and how much traffic a network can carry before the queues get out of hand, is the mathematics Leonard Kleinrock, Charley's boss at UCLA, had worked out.

A myth, and what came next

You may have heard that the internet was built to survive a nuclear war. Baran's RAND work really was about communications that could survive an attack. But the people who built ARPANET, writing the Internet Society's history, call the link a "false rumor": ARPANET was built to share computers.

The network's rules were written as open memos called Requests for Comments, and still are: more than nine thousand of them. Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn designed new rules to join separate networks into one network of networks, and on 1 January 1983 the ARPANET switched to them for good: TCP/IP. It kept the packets and moved the checking and resending to the computers at the two ends.

What went wrong at SRI

Charley Kline later put it simply: Bill had a bug, and it crashed. The exact bug was never written down, and later retellings of its cause don't agree, so we leave it there. Bill fixed it that same night, and about an hour after the crash the full login went through. Charley wrote one line in the UCLA log: "talked to SRI host to host". Leonard Kleinrock later said the crash had picked a fitting first word: lo, as in "lo and behold".

The first message

On the twenty-ninth of October, nineteen sixty-nine, a student at UCLA typed the first internet message. He meant to type a whole word: login. The computer at the other end crashed after two letters. So the first message on the network that became the internet was just L, O. Every message you send today still travels the way those two letters did: cut into small pieces called packets. So let's follow one letter all the way, and find out why the other computer fell over.

Two computers, 350 miles apart

The network was called ARPANET, and it was paid for by the research agency of the US Defense Department. Its goal was practical: let researchers in one place use expensive computers somewhere else. The first two sites were UCLA, in Los Angeles, and the Stanford Research Institute, about three hundred and fifty miles north. Each site had its own big computer, and the two had never been connected. Next to each one stood a new cabinet called an IMP: an Interface Message Processor. A company called BBN built them, from a Honeywell 516 minicomputer. It had about twelve thousand words of memory, and its whole job was moving messages. UCLA got the first IMP in September. SRI got the second, about a month later. A leased phone line joined the two IMPs, carrying fifty thousand bits a second. And alongside the computers, the two programmers were on an ordinary phone call. At UCLA, the student Charley Kline. At SRI, the programmer Bill Duvall.

Follow one letter

Charley's job that evening was to log in to the SRI computer from UCLA, as if he were sitting in front of it. He typed the first letter: L. The UCLA computer passed the letter to its IMP. The IMP wrapped it in a message with a header on the front that said where it was going. Then it sent that message up the line, one bit at a time. At SRI, the second IMP checked that the message had arrived intact, and sent back a note saying it got it. Then it handed the letter to its computer. On the phone, Charley asked: did you get the L? Bill said: got the L. He typed the O. Same journey, same answer: got the O. Then he typed the G, and the computer at SRI crashed. We'll come back to what went wrong at SRI. First, look at what kept working.

What kept working

Every letter arrived. The IMPs and the line did their job. The crash was in the computer at the far end. And the IMPs were built expecting the line itself to make mistakes. A long-distance phone line could flip a bit now and then, turning a one into a zero. So every packet carried check digits: a small number worked out from all of its bits. The receiving IMP did the same calculation. If the numbers didn't match, it threw the message away, and the sending IMP, hearing no acknowledgement, sent it again. Errors were expected, and repaired, without anyone at either keyboard noticing.

Why packets

That network in the middle worked differently from the phone network it borrowed its line from. A phone call in nineteen sixty-nine held a whole circuit open, end to end, for as long as you talked. Even in the silences, nobody else could use that line. Computers don't send like people talk. They send a short burst, then nothing, then another burst. In the nineteen sixties, two researchers reached the same answer independently: Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation in America, and Donald Davies at Britain's National Physical Laboratory. Cut every message into small blocks. Put the destination on each block. Let every block find its own way through the network. Davies gave those blocks the name we still use today: packets. Now many conversations can share the same line, and if one route breaks, packets can take another.

Store and forward

Inside the network, every IMP on the route does the same three things. It takes in the whole packet and stores it. It checks it, and passes it on to the next IMP. And it keeps its own copy until the next one says: got it. Only then does it let go. When the outgoing line is busy, packets wait their turn in a queue inside the IMP. How long they wait, and how much traffic a network can carry before the queues get out of hand, is the mathematics Charley's boss at UCLA, Leonard Kleinrock, had worked out.

A myth, and what came next

You might have heard that the internet was built to survive a nuclear war. Baran's work at RAND really was about communications that could survive an attack. But the people who built ARPANET have called that link a false rumour. ARPANET was built to share computers. By December nineteen sixty-nine, four computers were on the network: UCLA, SRI, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah. Its rules were written as open memos, called Requests for Comments. They still are, more than nine thousand of them. Meanwhile, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn designed new rules to join many separate networks into one network of networks. On the first of January, nineteen eighty-three, the ARPANET switched over to those rules for good: TCP/IP. It kept the packets, and moved the checking and resending out to the computers at the two ends: if a packet goes missing, they notice, and it gets sent again. That's how this video reached you: thousands of packets, each finding its own way, and any that went missing quietly sent again.

What went wrong at SRI

So what went wrong at SRI? Charley Kline later put it simply: Bill had a bug, and it crashed. The exact bug never made it into the log. Bill fixed it that same night. About an hour after the crash, Charley logged in to SRI from UCLA. The whole word went through. And in the UCLA log book, he wrote one line: talked to SRI, host to host.
