The story in 60 seconds

AT&T's long-distance backbone was 114 computer switches, called 4ESS, that watched each other's health. If one went quiet, its neighbours routed calls around it. In December 1989, new software went into all 114 switches at once.

At about 2:25 PM on 15 January, a switch in New York City reset itself, as designed. When it came back, it sent its neighbours a quick burst of messages. A neighbour running the new code got a second message before it had finished with the first — and that is where the bug lived. It scrambled its own records, reset, came back, and sent its own burst. As AT&T's Larry Seese put it a week later: "It was a chain reaction."

The cruel part: the bug only fired when two messages arrived close together — exactly what a recovering switch sends. The recovery itself was the trigger.

How engineers stopped it

Every switch that came back simply restarted the wave, and as more switches fell, the survivors got busier — making two messages landing close together even more likely. So the engineers did the opposite of pushing harder: they cut out the backup signalling network, the flood of messages roughly halved, and the switches began to stay up. The last switch cleared at half past eleven that night.

The first clue to the cause came from the switches that didn't fall: stations still running an older generation of the software kept working through the chaos. Pinning down the exact line took a couple of weeks, because the bug only appeared when two messages landed within about a hundredth of a second of each other.

Three lessons from one line of code

  1. Try a change in one place before it goes everywhere — a "canary" rollout.
  2. If every machine runs identical code, they can all fail together.
  3. Make recovery wait its turn, so coming back doesn't knock your neighbour over.

The same shape keeps returning: CrowdStrike's faulty update in July 2024, Facebook's backbone outage in October 2021, and the Microsoft 365 outage in November 2026 all involved a safety or recovery system that ended up spreading the failure.

One line of code, nine hours of failed calls

This is the 1990 AT&T crash, and it was started by one line of code. On a Monday afternoon in January, America's biggest long-distance phone network began knocking itself out, one switch at a time. For about nine hours, roughly half of the long-distance calls people tried to make simply failed. There was no fire, no storm and no attack. The network had been built so that no single failure could take it down, and its own safety system is what spread the damage. In the next ten minutes: how one tiny mistake did this, how engineers stopped it, and why the same pattern still takes down the systems you use today.

A network built never to fail

In 1990, a call across the country ran through AT&T's long-distance network. Its backbone was 114 giant computer switches, called 4ESS, spread across the United States. Each one could connect up to seven hundred thousand calls an hour. Alongside the calls ran a second, hidden network that the switches used to talk to each other about those calls. And the switches kept an eye on each other's health. If one got overloaded or went quiet, its neighbours noticed and quietly sent calls another way. Think of hikers on a mountain trail, calling "I'm OK" down the line: if one goes silent, the group simply routes around them. It was designed so that no one switch could ever bring the whole system down. And on most days, it worked perfectly.

The update nobody noticed

In December 1989, AT&T engineers loaded new software into the switches. It was meant to make them handle certain messages faster. It had been through testing, and it went into all 114 switches. Imagine every referee in a league getting the same new rulebook on the same day: if there's a typo in it, it's in every match at once. Through the busy Christmas season, nothing went wrong. The mistake just sat there, waiting for one very specific moment.

The wave: 2:25 PM, 15 January 1990

That moment came at about 2:25 PM on Monday, 15 January 1990, which was Martin Luther King Day. A switch in New York City hit a problem with some of its equipment, and did exactly what it was designed to do: it reset itself. A reset like that takes about four to six seconds. While it was down, it told its neighbours: don't send me any calls. Then it came back, and started sending out its messages again, in a quick burst.

In another city, a neighbouring switch got the first message and began updating its notes: New York is back. Then a second message arrived, just a few thousandths of a second later, before it had finished with the first. That's where the bug lived: the second message scrambled the switch's own records, so it shut itself down and reset. A few seconds later it came back, and sent its own burst of messages to its neighbours. As AT&T's Larry Seese put it a week later: "It was a chain reaction." It was a stadium wave nobody could stop: each section stands up only because the one beside it just did. Within about ten minutes, the wave had crossed the country. In Seese's words, "The event just repeated itself in every switch, over and over again."

The bug: one misplaced break

So what was the mistake? It was in code written in C. Here's a simplified version of it, as it was described in 1990. When a message arrives, the switch asks: was the sender out of service? If it was, and the switch isn't busy, it marks the sender as back in service. But if the switch is busy, the code says one word: break. The programmer meant: skip this step, and carry on with the message. But in C, that break means: leave this whole block of code, right now. So the program skipped the line that set everything up, and started writing over data it still needed. It's like a sat-nav telling you to exit one junction too early: every instruction after that is right, but you're on the wrong road.

The switch's own safety checks spotted the damage and did the sensible thing: shut down and start again. As AT&T described it, the switch was effectively saying: "My CCS7 processor is insane." And here's the cruel part: the bug only fired when two messages arrived close together, which is exactly what a recovering switch sends. Seese again: "If the messages had been received farther apart, it would not have triggered the problem." The recovery itself was the trigger.

The first suspects

Now picture being in charge that afternoon. The nation's long-distance network is failing on a public holiday, and nobody yet knows why. Some in law enforcement and telephone security suspected hackers. Rumours of hidden viruses and logic bombs in the switches spread fast. It's like coming home to a flooded house and assuming a burglar, when it was a pipe you fitted yourself last month. Soon after, AT&T said the problem was traced to a single computer program: its own. Still, that cloud of suspicion hung over the hacker raids that followed later that year.

How engineers stopped it

In AT&T's network operations centre in New Jersey, a wall of screens was lit up red. Bell Labs engineers in New Jersey, Illinois and Ohio tried every standard remedy they had, and none of them worked. Every switch that came back simply started the wave all over again. And it was feeding itself: as more switches fell, the survivors got busier, which made two messages landing close together even more likely. So the engineers did the opposite of pushing harder: they cut out the backup signalling network entirely, and the flood of "I'm OK" messages roughly halved. Fewer messages meant fewer pairs arriving too close together, and the switches began to stay up. It's like a jammed motorway where you close the on-ramps: the cars already on the road clear, then you let the rest in slowly. At half past eleven that night, the last switch cleared. Tens of millions of calls had failed.

The bug hunt

But stopping the wave isn't the same as knowing what started it. Nobody that night could see the bug we just watched in slow motion. The first clue came from the switches that didn't fall. Some stations still ran an older generation of the software, and they kept working right through the chaos. Same calls, same day, same storm of messages: the difference was the new code. It's like a power cut where only the houses with the new wiring lose their lights: you know where to look.

Within nine hours, AT&T's engineers more or less understood what had happened. By the next day, they were pulling the new software out of the switches and putting back an earlier version. But "more or less" isn't good enough when a whole country's phones are at stake. They had to make the crash happen again, on purpose, and catch the exact line in the act. That's hard, because this bug only appeared when two messages landed within about a hundredth of a second of each other. Replicating it exactly, and poring over the software line by line, took them a couple of weeks. Bugs that only show up with perfect timing are still some of the hardest that engineers ever hunt. Meanwhile, AT&T's boss, Bob Allen, ran an apology in newspapers across the country: "We didn't live up to our own standards of quality, and we didn't live up to yours." And as a peace offering, AT&T gave customers a discount on Valentine's Day.

It still happens: CrowdStrike, Facebook, Microsoft

36 years later, the code is different, but the pattern keeps coming back. July 2024: one faulty security update from CrowdStrike crashed about 8.5 million Windows computers, many of them running critical services. CrowdStrike now releases updates like that in stages. October 2021: a command meant only to check Facebook's network capacity took its whole backbone offline. And when engineers got it back, they brought traffic back in stages, because switching everything on at once could cause a new round of crashes. And this month, Microsoft 365 went down: servers that failed automated health checks dropped out, and their traffic overloaded the rest. Different bugs, same shape: a safety system built to route around failure ended up spreading it. That's why careful engineers use a canary: like miners who carried a bird into the mine, they send a change to one small group first and watch it before anyone else gets it.

Three lessons from one line of code

So, three lessons from one line of code. One: try a change in one place before it goes everywhere. Two: if every machine runs identical code, they can all fail together. Three: make recovery wait its turn, so coming back doesn't knock your neighbour over. Somewhere in the systems you rely on today, there's a line of code that just hasn't met its moment yet.
